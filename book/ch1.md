# The Light Goes Flat at Half Past Three

Fifteen forty. Bober is forty feet up in a steel basket, and the can is sitting on the rail by his left knee, unopened.

The wall behind him is two-thirds finished. Below the basket there is a pallet of paint, a generator, a man in a hard hat who is legally responsible for the machine Bober is standing in, and a strip of pavement taped off with the kind of plastic ribbon that is the only ribbon in this book that ever meant anything. Bober has been up here since half past seven. His hands are the colour of the wall. He has not opened the can.

This is the moment the whole book is about, so let us not spend it badly.

A lesser book gives you an anecdote here. A lesser book tells you that the Polish painter reached for a beer, and then does a little sad music about the Polish painter reaching for a beer, and the reader nods, because everyone already knows the tune. The tune is the enemy. The tune arrives before the facts do, it explains everything, and it is wrong -- Chapter 2 will take a table of numbers to it and leave nothing standing.

So before anybody opens anything, here is the apparatus. Every number in it is real.

## The wall has a temperature

Bober is not painting a picture. He is applying an industrial coating to the exterior of a residential building, and the coating has opinions.

Acrylic facade paint wants to go on between plus 10 and plus 30 degrees Celsius. Silicone facade paint is the more forgiving of the two and will work down to plus 5. Below that, nothing goes on the wall that you would want to see next spring. Rain is out. Wind is out, because wind takes the spray and gives it to somebody's parked car. Frost is out. High humidity is out. Harsh direct sun is also out, which surprises people who assume the enemy is only cold: sun flashes the surface dry before the film has knitted, and you get a wall that looks finished and is not.

Add those constraints together across a Polish year and you get an exterior painting season of roughly six to seven months. Half the calendar is simply unavailable. Not difficult -- unavailable. The wall does not care about the festival's programme, the grant's reporting deadline, or the fact that the artist is at the height of his powers in February.

Then there is the volume. A gable end of a thousand square metres takes something like 170 to 330 litres of base paint to get two coats on, and that is before a single can of anything with a colour in it is touched. Before the image. Before the thing anyone photographs. Two or three bathtubs of white, lifted, hauled, decanted, rolled, and then repeated, because one coat is not a coat.

Scale is the point. When you stand on the pavement and look up at a mural, you are looking at a painting. When you stand in the basket, you are looking at a surface, and the surface is winning.

## The certificate

The basket has a name: podnosnik koszowy. A boom lift. On a tall building it is not a preference, it is the only economic option, because scaffolding a twelve-storey gable to paint it costs more than the painting.

The man operating the lift must hold UDT certification. Not "should." Must. There is a state technical inspection office, there is a certificate, there is a human being who has passed an examination in order to raise a painter to the height at which the painter can be an artist. Somebody in this arrangement holds a formal qualification, and it is not the one with paint in his eyebrows.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the first joke this book actually believes in. The romantic account of the muralist has him alone with the wall, the city, and his demons. The real account has him standing in a certified machine driven by a certified operator inside a taped-off exclusion zone, subject to a weather window, applying a product with a datasheet. The demons, if they show up, have to clear it with the site.

## How long a wall takes

Fifty to a hundred square metres runs four to ten days. That is a modest wall -- a single gable of a low block, the sort of thing a passer-by processes in a second and a half.

Bigger, then. In Lodz in October 2021, a twenty-four-storey wall over seventy metres high took eight painters more than two weeks. Eight. More than two weeks. And in the same city in 2013, the Chilean painter Inti did an eleven-storey wall over eleven days, alone.

Eleven days. Alone. On a machine. In the weather.

Whatever you think is happening to a person's attention on day nine of eleven at altitude, it is not the thing that happens to a person's attention in a studio. There is no back of the room to walk to. You cannot step away from the work, because the work is eleven storeys tall and you are strapped to it. To see what you have done, you have to descend, cross the road, turn round, look, cross back, and go up again -- which costs minutes and daylight, so you ration it, so you spend most of the day painting something you cannot see.

This is the physical fact that the entire mythology of the drinking artist has never had to account for. It is a job. It has a lift, an operator, a datasheet, a window, and a clock.

## The eye goes before the arm

Here is why the can is still shut at half past three and why it will not be shut at twenty to four.

Bober has been mixing and matching colour on a vertical surface in daylight for eight hours. The arm is fine. The arm could go until dark. The eye is what fails, and it fails in a specific, boring, physical way: the low sun comes round, the light loses its bite, and the colours on the wall stop being distinguishable from one another. Everything goes slightly the same. The shadow he mixed at eleven and the shadow he is mixing now are two different shadows that currently look like one shadow, and he will only find out which at ten past nine tomorrow morning, on somebody else's daylight.

A painter can work with a tired arm. A painter cannot work with a lying eye. Every mark laid down after the light flattens is a mark that has a decent chance of having to come off tomorrow -- and coming off, on a facade, means sanding back and repainting a section you already paid for twice.

So the working day does not end when Bober is finished. It ends when the light stops telling the truth. That moment arrives in the middle of the afternoon, and it arrives whether or not he is ready, whether or not the section is complete, whether or not the festival wants the wall done by Friday.

Now open the can.

It is a beer. It cost about as much as a bread roll. It is 4.7 percent, in a 500 millilitre bottle or a can with a blue ribbon printed on it, and the ribbon is a lie of such magnificent construction that it gets its own chapter later. He drinks it slowly, because it is the only thing today he is permitted to do slowly.

The comedy is in the mismatch. Certified operator, industrial coating, thirty-metre boom, exclusion zone, six-month season, 330 litres of base, eight painters and two weeks -- and at the top of this entire apparatus, the object that ends the working day is a supermarket lager that a Polish beer site once reviewed and gave two out of ten.

## What the beer is not doing

Note the time again, because it does more work than any adjective could.

It is not the morning. This matters, and the book will keep insisting on it. Bober does not drink to get up the ladder, does not drink to steady the hand at eight, does not need a klin to start. The first one lands after eight hours of work, at the precise point in the day when the specific faculty his trade depends on has stopped functioning. It arrives at the end of the useful part of the day, not the beginning of it.

That timing changes the question completely. "Why does Bober drink beer?" sounds like a question about a man's character. Timed to the light, it becomes a question about a working day: what is the object that tells a man on a lift that the day's judgement is finished, and why does it have to be an object at all? Nothing else up there can say it. The wall cannot say it -- the wall is two-thirds done and looks reproachful in any light. The client cannot say it. The forecast, which will be worse tomorrow, definitely cannot say it.

There is a Polish line that convicts the can from the other direction, and it is the best five words anyone has ever written on the subject. In Smarzowski's WESELE, a character says of vodka: "Poniewierac ma, a nie smakowac." It is meant to knock you about, not to taste good.

That is a whole philosophy, and it has no room in it for what Bober is holding. A 4.7 percent lager, one of them, sipped over ten minutes at altitude, is not knocking anybody about. By the strictest available Polish theory of drinking, this barely counts as drinking. The folk couplet makes the same distinction and even ranks it: "Wszak od wodki rozum krotki, a od piwa glowa sie kiwa." From vodka, short wits; from beer, a nodding head. Two substances, two verdicts, and Bober is holding the second one -- the one that gets you a nodding head, not a short wit.

Anyone who wants to explain this man with the phrase "Poles drink" now has to explain why he is drinking the wrong thing, at the wrong hour, in the wrong quantity, in a country the numbers say is eighth and falling.

## The vocabulary rule, stated once

There is a Polish phrase that will run under the rest of this book: przy piwie. Over a beer. It is the standard frame for the conversation where somebody finally says what they think -- the politics, the grievance, the thing that could not be said in the meeting. Not at a beer, not with a beer. Over one. The beer is the setting, the licence and the alibi in a single preposition.

Here is the discipline, and it is worth stating in the first chapter so that nobody has to wonder later. Every Polish word in this book is one that a dictionary, a printed source or a named speaker will stand behind. Where the research could not confirm a phrase, the phrase is absent, however good it would have sounded. A book about a stereotype cannot afford to run on invented folklore, because invented folklore is exactly the machinery that built the stereotype. If it is not verified, it is not here.

## In every normal country

Ziemowit Szczerek, who has spent a career on the ugly and specific truth of Polish public space, opens a passage in his novel Siodemka with this: "W kazdym normalnym kraju, od tego zacznijmy, nie uzywa sie zwrotu 'w kazdym normalnym kraju'." In every normal country, let's start here, nobody uses the phrase "in every normal country."

That sentence is this book's licence. The instinct to measure yourself against an imagined normal country is the same instinct that answers a question about one man on a lift with a fact about a nation. In every normal country a man has a beer after work. In every normal country nobody would write a book about it. Both of those sentences are ways of not looking at the thing.

So we are going to look at the thing, and we are going to take a stupid-sounding question with more seriousness than it has ever been shown, on the grounds that this is the only way anybody finds out anything.

Here is what the question has to survive.

It has to survive the numbers, which say Poland is eighth in Europe and falling, that the champion of Central Europe is Czechia by two full litres, and that the country in the stereotype has been quietly turning into a beer country for thirty years. That is the next chapter, and it removes national character from the list of available answers.

Then it has to survive the history, which will show that nobody in Poland ever chose this supply -- that a landlord held the legal right to make the alcohol, the monopoly on selling it, and the power to compel a household to buy a quota, with the unbought remainder dumped in front of the house and charged for. Six centuries of somebody else building the reflex, and a modern parliament still adjusting the dial.

Then it has to survive the can itself, which is a Polish product wearing an American costume, decorated with a prize that was never awarded, and endorsed by a science that did not replicate.

And when all four of those answers have been taken away, something will still be true at twenty to four in the afternoon, forty feet up, with the light going flat and the wall two-thirds finished.

The apparatus is certified. The paint has a datasheet. The season is six months. The operator holds a licence to lift him.

Nobody has issued a certificate for stopping. That is what the can is for, and it takes four more chapters to prove it.
