# Eighth Place, and Falling

Eighth.

That is the whole chapter, and everything after this sentence is just the eighth place being carried up four flights of stairs and set down gently in front of you so you can see it is real. Poland is eighth in Europe for alcohol consumption. Not first. Not second. Not, as roughly every bar conversation in the English-speaking world has assumed since about 1981, so far out in front that the second-place country needs a telescope. Eighth. Behind Romania, Latvia, Portugal, Czechia, Bulgaria, Spain and Austria, in that order, with Hungary, Lithuania and France breathing on the back of its neck.

The joke you were going to make does not survive the table. Let us put the table on the page and watch it die.

## The Table

WHO Global Health Observatory, indicator SDG 3.5.2, total alcohol per capita consumption among adults aged fifteen and over, three-year average, 2024 figures, in litres of pure ethanol:

Romania 17.07. Latvia 14.18. Portugal 13.75. Czechia 13.52. Bulgaria 13.25. Spain 12.07. Austria 11.60. **Poland 11.51.** Hungary 11.36. Lithuania 11.23. France 11.19. Germany 10.93. Ireland 10.27.

Romania beats Poland by 5.56 litres of pure alcohol per adult per year. That is not a rounding error, that is a rout. Czechia -- Central Europe's actual champion, a country whose national self-image is a beer glass with a head on it and which has the receipts to back it -- beats Poland by 2.01. Portugal, a nation the drunken-Pole joke has never once considered, is 2.24 litres ahead and doing it mostly in wine, in the sun, with lunch.

And look where the joke's usual straight man ends up. Germany, the country invoked in every comparison as the sensible one, the one with the engineering and the punctuality, is 10.93. Poland is 11.51. The gap is 0.58 litres a year. That is about one and a half glasses of wine. Twelve months of national character, and the difference between the stereotype and its own control group is a glass and a half of wine.

If the drunken Pole is a real category of person, he is beaten annually by a Romanian, a Latvian, a Portuguese, a Czech, a Bulgarian, a Spaniard and an Austrian, and he is barely holding off a Hungarian, a Lithuanian and a Frenchman. He is a mid-table finisher. He is the guy who comes eighth and is quietly pleased not to have come tenth.

Nobody makes jokes about the drunken Latvian. Latvia is 14.18.

## And Falling

Eighth would be a bad enough result for the stereotype if it were stable. It is not stable. It is going down.

WHO's recorded figure for Poland -- the alcohol that legally exists, that was made and taxed and rung through a till -- peaked in the modern era at 10.99 in 2019. By 2023 it was 10.00. In 2024 it was 9.79. Across the whole decade from 2013 to 2023 the number moved from 10.81 to 10.00. That is a country drinking less, year after year, through a period in which the world was mostly finding new reasons to drink more.

The beer market agrees. Polish beer went from 41 million hectolitres in 2018 to 34.6 million in 2024, a collapse the trade press described as the equivalent of six large breweries closing. Beer in natural litres per head peaked at 100.5 in 2018 and is now around 85.8. The industry is not sitting on a national vice, it is sitting on a shrinking one, watching fifteen litres a head walk out the door and not come back.

So the stereotype is wrong about the level and wrong about the direction. It describes a country that is drinking more than everyone else and getting worse, and the country in question is drinking less than seven other countries and getting better. As predictions go, this is the weather forecast calling for a heatwave on a wall that will not take paint below plus ten.

## Two Honest Corrections, Because a Debunking That Cheats Is Just a Second Lie

Two things have to be said here or this chapter is no better than the thing it is attacking.

First: this is not the highest Polish drinking has ever been, and anyone telling you the country is at some unprecedented historical summit is selling something. The all-time recorded peak was 1981, at 11.21 -- higher than 2019's 10.99 -- and then martial law arrived and the figure fell off a cliff to 8.54 in 1982, for reasons that had nothing to do with anybody's willpower and everything to do with tanks. Hold that thought. It comes back in the next chapter with a vengeance.

Second: there are two numbers for Polish drinking and they must never be mixed. The Polish domestic series, from GUS and KCPU, counts registered sales across the whole population, infants included, and gives 8.8 litres for 2024. WHO counts adults from fifteen up and adds an estimate for unrecorded alcohol, and gives 9.79 recorded, 11.51 total. Both are correct. They are measuring different things with different denominators. Every alarmist headline about Polish drinking that you have ever read was built by taking the big number from one column and the scary adjective from another, and the honest version of this argument does not get to do that either. Eleven point five one is the number that puts Poland eighth in Europe. Eight point eight is the number that describes the average Polish resident, newborns and abstainers and grandmothers included. Neither of them is first place.

The domestic series, incidentally, begins in 1992. If somebody quotes you a Polish government figure for 1990, they have invented it. It does not exist.

## Bober Is Not Even Drinking Vodka

Here is where the stereotype stops being merely wrong about quantity and starts being wrong about the object.

The joke is always vodka. It is a shot glass, a bottle on a table, something clear and forty percent, and a shudder. That picture was defensible once. In 1990, spirits were 5.06 litres of Poland's per-adult intake, sixty-two percent of everything drunk. Beer was 2.02 litres, twenty-four point eight percent. The country really was, by volume of pure alcohol, a vodka country, and the cartoon had a nation behind it.

By 2000, spirits were down to 2.47 litres and 30.8 percent. Beer was up to 3.78 and 47.1 percent. In one decade, the thing the joke was about lost half its share.

By 2024, spirits have come back up to 3.99 litres and 40.8 percent -- and that partial return is not a story about character either, it is a story about a tax cut, which is Chapter 3's business -- but beer is now 5.04 litres and 51.5 percent. Beer, in natural litres per head, went from 38.6 in 1992 to 100.5 at the 2018 peak. Poland spent thirty years quietly turning into a beer country while the rest of the world kept telling the vodka joke.

Which brings us up the wall. Bober is forty feet up on a boom lift with a wall two-thirds finished and the light going flat, and in his hand is a five hundred millilitre pale lager at 4.7 percent. Not a setka. Not a malpka. Not a bottle of anything clear. He is holding the majority beverage of a mid-table European country, and he is holding it in the exact proportion the national statistics predict.

The folk couplet knew the difference before the statisticians did. *Wszak od wodki rozum krotki, a od piwa glowa sie kiwa.* From vodka, short wits; from beer, a nodding head. Two substances, two consequences, one couplet, and the joke about Poland has spent forty years insisting on the first half while the country migrated into the second.

## The Costume, Worn by the People It Supposedly Describes

Now the part that makes the stereotype genuinely funny rather than merely false.

Poles are better at the drunken-Pole joke than anyone else alive. It is not close. Foreigners have a cartoon. Poland has a literature, a dictionary and a national repertoire, and all of it is sharper, filthier and more self-lacerating than anything a visitor has ever managed.

Consider what is codified in the actual PAN dictionaries, not folklore, not vibes, but headwords. *Kolega od kieliszka:* a person with whom close acquaintance rests on meetings at which alcohol is drunk. Polish has a dictionary entry for the friend you only have because you drink together. *Pic do lustra*, to drink to the mirror, meaning to drink alone. *Zalac robaka*, to drown the worm. *Urwal sie film*, the film broke -- and grammatically the film is the subject and it breaks on you, in the dative, so that even the blackout is something that happens to a man rather than something he does. *Wchodzic miedzy wodke a zakaske*, to get between the vodka and the chaser, meaning to butt in: Polish built an idiom for interfering out of the drinking sequence itself. *Bez wodki nie rozbierzesz*, you cannot work it out without vodka, said of anything too tangled to understand sober, and it has nine registered variants, several in mock-Russian. *Trzezwy jak swinia*, sober as a pig, an anti-idiom that exists purely to be funny about the real one. And for the hangover, alongside plain *kac*, the WSJP offers *tupot bialych mew* -- the patter of white seagulls.

A country that has produced the phrase "the patter of white seagulls" for a hangover does not need a foreigner's help.

And then there is Pilch. *Pod Mocnym Aniolem*, 1999, Nike Prize, and the passage that ought to be printed on the back of every drunken-Pole joke ever made:

*"Pili, bo Polska byla pod moskiewskim jarzmem, i pili w euforii po wyzwoleniu. Pili, bo Polak zostal papiezem... Pili, gdy byli sami, i pili, gdy ktokolwiek pojawil sie obok nich. Pili, kiedy Polska zwyciezala, i pili, kiedy Polska przegrywala."*

They drank because Poland was under the Moscow yoke, and they drank in the euphoria after liberation. They drank because a Pole became Pope. They drank when they were alone and they drank when anyone at all appeared beside them. They drank when Poland won and they drank when Poland lost.

That is the stereotype, written better than any outsider has ever written it, by a Pole, in Polish, in a prize-winning novel, in a country that ranks eighth. The definitive statement of the national drinking myth was composed inside the nation, for domestic consumption, as art. It is a costume, and the Poles keep it in the wardrobe and put it on for guests, and they wear it better than the guests do because they tailored it themselves.

The self-justification is just as old and just as knowing. *Nie my pierwsi obyczaj picia wprowadzili, pili Grecy, Rzymianie i Sarmaci pili.* We were not the first to bring in the custom of drinking; the Greeks drank, the Romans drank, and the Sarmatians drank. Four hundred years ago somebody was already doing the bit about how this is not really our fault, and doing it with a classical education.

Michal R. Wisniewski, writing in 2014 about gonzo journalism and its Polish practitioners, put the mechanism in one clause. Gonzo, he said, "makes such an impression on people, like, at last, some truth -- and it's never the truth." That is the whole architecture of the drunken-Pole joke. It arrives with the swagger of candour. It feels like somebody finally saying the unsayable thing. And it is a number that is eighth and falling, dressed up as a revelation.

## The One Thing That Survives

None of which means the subject is harmless, and the book will say this once, plainly, and then leave it alone.

Between 1999 and 2022, Poland recorded roughly 79,000 deaths from alcoholic liver cirrhosis. Among men aged forty-five to sixty-four, the annual figure went from 436 to 2,583. Among women aged sixty-five and over, from 11 to 436.

That is the statistic that survives the debunking. It does not restore the stereotype -- it has nothing to do with national character, and countries ranked well above Poland have their own versions of it -- but it is the reason none of this is a game. It is on the page. It stays on the page. We move on, because the man on the lift would.

## What Is Left

So the lazy answer is gone. Bober does not drink beer at half past three because he is Polish. Polishness, measured in the only unit that measures it, puts him eighth in Europe, below Austria, and improving. If national character were the mechanism, the Romanians would be the punchline and the Latvians would be the folk tale, and the joke would have moved to Bucharest decades ago.

The stereotype was never a description. It was a costume, cut and stitched in Poland, sold abroad, and worn back home for laughs by people who own the pattern.

Which leaves the question exactly where Chapter 1 put it, only harder. A man on a boom lift, forty feet up, at a precise hour of a working day, opening a specific object. The reflex is real. The timing is real. The national explanation is a table with Poland in eighth place on it.

Somebody built this. Not the Poles -- the supply.

Six centuries of somebodies, in fact, and every one of them had a ledger.
