# A Prize Nobody Won, A Muse Nobody Found

## Read It Out Loud As A Document

Forty feet up, the wall two-thirds done, Bober holds the can out at arm's length and squints at it the way he squints at a colour he does not trust.

It is a document. Treat it like one. A blue ribbon on the front, which is a picture of an award. The date 1844, which is a founding, not a victory. A sentence, in the small heraldic type these sentences always come in, saying the beer was selected as America's best in 1893. And near the bottom, in four-point grey, the small print and the barcode, naming a brewery in Podkarpackie.

Four claims. The ribbon commemorates a prize that was never given. The slogan describes a competition that did not work the way the slogan says. The country of origin is a licence agreement. Only the founding date is true, and it is true about a company that no longer brews the thing in his hand. Every claim on that can is either a lie or a foreign address.

Bober knows none of this and it would not change his afternoon if he did. This chapter is about the two flattering stories attached to the object -- that it is a winner, and that it helps him make things -- and how both failed in the same way.

## The Prize Nobody Won

Pabst tied real blue silk ribbons around the necks of its bottles from 1882. Actual silk, tied by hand. The Pabst Mansion puts the volume at one million feet of ribbon a year. It was retail decoration, a way of making a bottle look dressed the way a shop window is dressed, and it had been doing that job for eleven years before anybody held the fair the ribbon is now said to commemorate.

Now the fair. The 1893 World's Columbian Exposition awarded no ribbons at all -- not to Pabst, not to anyone. The judging was not a contest in the sporting sense: no head-to-head, no final, no runner-up. Entries were scored against a fixed written standard, and every beer scoring 80 percent or above received an identical bronze medal designed by St Gaudens. No gold. No silver. No grand prize. Pabst got the same medal as everybody else who cleared the bar, then marketed itself as though there had been only one.

This came from the 1891 contest rules and Bancroft's contemporaneous *Book of the Fair* -- the paperwork of the event itself, always there, never read against the advertising. When Dr Loih published it in 2017, the Pabst Mansion revised its website. So did the Wisconsin Historical Society. Two institutions whose entire function is to be right about Milwaukee had been repeating the beer's own press release.

And the sentence itself. "Selected as America's Best in 1893" was written in the 1950s, by a Pabst that was struggling and looking through its own attic for something to say. Sixty years separate the event from the claim about the event.

In order: the ribbon predates the prize by eleven years, the prize did not exist, and the sentence announcing it was composed six decades after the non-event, by people who needed a story more than they needed the truth. Nothing here is a scandal. It is an ordinary piece of commercial fiction that hardened, the way a rendered wall hardens, into something everyone treats as load-bearing.

## An Anti-Brand Is Still A Brand

The second story is better, because Pabst did not write it. Pabst found it.

In 1985 Paul Kalmanovitz bought the company for 63 million dollars, and his method was that of a man buying a dying animal for its hide: acquire fading brands, cut advertising to zero, cut quality control, milk the customers who would buy it anyway out of habit. The beloved fact that PBR does not advertise did not begin as a philosophy. It began as neglect with a spreadsheet behind it. By 1996 the Milwaukee plant was closed and Pabst was a "virtual brewer," a trademark with no brewery. By 2001 it was below a million barrels.

Then, in 2002, sales spiked in Portland, Oregon, with no marketing whatsoever behind them, and the brand manager Neal Stewart went to find out why. The turnaround was not designed. It was noticed. Somebody was already drinking the beer for reasons of their own, and a company out of ideas had the wit to go and ask.

What he found was a customer with a threat in his mouth. Stewart, 2004: "They literally said to us, 'I love your beer but the minute you advertise on TV, I'm done with you.'" And of the brand he inherited: "It was a forgotten brand with no marketing support."

So Pabst spent money on being absent. No TV, no NFL, no FM radio. Instead: bike messenger races, scooter rallies, kickball, fashion shows, and -- this matters here -- art exhibition openings, with beer and hats delivered to the gallery. Weekly quarter-page ads in the alternative papers of about 25 markets, each listing local bands and never the beer. MarketingSherpa called it slipping money in the manner of a sly uncle to these small events. Steve Nilsen, lifestyle marketing manager from 2009 to 2018, put the doctrine plainly: "We never told consumers -- ever -- what Pabst was: we let them decide for themselves." And: "Brands would pay big money to be at Coachella or South by Southwest or X Games. We were at all those events, but we didn't pay a dime to be there."

2002 was the first volume increase since 1978. 2003 was up 15 percent.

The company did not manufacture a meaning and broadcast it. It manufactured a vacancy and let its customers furnish it. A costume with the sleeves left unfinished, so the wearer takes them in himself and feels he tailored it.

## Pabst Pays People Who Do Bober's Job

Since 2014 there has been the PBR Art Can Contest. Over 300 million 16-ounce cans have carried contest art; over 100,000 dollars has gone to emerging artists. The first winner, in 2014, was Josh Holland. The 2021 winner was Ashley Dreyfus, a mixed-media artist from Boise, whose design ran on more than 30 million cans. In 2022 Hannah Bunzey won against roughly 7,000 entrants from more than 120 countries.

Bober is a mixed-media artist.

The circle closes so neatly it feels rude to point at it. The beer's meaning is that artists like it, so the beer pays artists to design its packaging. The costume is crowdsourced from the exact people who will later be photographed wearing it -- a real commission with a real fee, which makes it both a decent thing to do for a painter's rent and the most efficient advertising ever devised, because it is not advertising, it is a job. A brand whose entire proposition is that it does not sell to you has 300 million cans of other people's paintings in circulation.

Also, simply funny: Pabst has sold cases as large as 180 cans. Nothing to add. It is just the number.

## Rakszawa, Wisconsin

Now the address.

The PBR that reached Poland was not shipped from anywhere. It was brewed in Poland, by Van Pur S.A., under licence from Pabst, at Rakszawa in Podkarpackie -- the country's largest independent brewer, five breweries, of which Rakszawa is the biggest at 1.75 million hectolitres a year. Van Pur's own line about that plant is glorious here: "Most of the beer brewed in Rakszawa is exported to highly demanding and distant foreign markets."

The launch was confirmed on 12 May 2020 by Tomasz Kopyra, Poland's best-known beer blogger: "kultowe piwo amerykanskich hipsterow, czyli Pabst Blue Ribbon juz nad Wisla w wykonaniu Van Pura" -- the cult beer of American hipsters, now on the Vistula, in Van Pur's rendition. In Van Pur's rendition. A cover version.

Pale lager, 4.7 percent, 500 ml, 3.99 to 4.59 zloty, in Polomarket, Carrefour, Frisco and Spar. Not import shops. The mainstream grocery aisle at the price of ordinary Polish lager, because that is what it is. The Polish beer press gave it 2 out of 10: "bardzo nieprzyjemny, slodki, landrynkowy jasny lager. Kukurydziano-miodowy." A very unpleasant, sweet, boiled-sweet pale lager. Corn and honey.

The same trademark in Germany runs about 3 euro for a 355 ml can -- 8.45 euro a litre. A York bottle shop sells it labelled "USA Trash Lager," which is at least honest about the purchase. And in China, Pabst sells "Blue Ribbon 1844": 720 ml, all-malt, reddish-brown, strong, dry-hopped with Cascade, aged in new uncharred American whiskey barrels, about 44 dollars a bottle, served with a champagne flute.

One trademark. Four class positions. A 44-dollar luxury ale, an 8.45-euro-a-litre affectation, a 4-zloty supermarket lager out of a Podkarpackie village, and a working-class signifier in Portland. The liquid is barely involved.

(Whether the Polish licence still runs in 2026 could not be confirmed, and the book will not pretend otherwise. What is solid: from 2020, PBR in Poland was Polish.)

Which is the title of this book and the joke at the centre of it. Bober is drinking Polish beer wearing an American costume. The most local possible object -- brewed by a Polish-owned company, sold in a Polish supermarket at a Polish price -- with the most foreign possible label glued to the front. Janusz, in Smarzowski's *Wesele*, on vodka: "Poniewierac ma, a nie smakowac." It's meant to knock you about, not to taste good. PBR fails that test deliberately, and the failure is the entire product.

## The Costume Is Also On Fire

And the company selling it is going under. Pabst was the fifth-largest US brewer in 2024 at 3.1 million barrels across all brands, down 5.6 percent on 2023. In 2025 it cut 18 percent of its workforce, replaced its CEO and CFO, then cut another 60 to 70 people in December. The head office is being sublet.

A brand whose only asset was not being sold has spent a decade trying to sell itself.

## The Muse Nobody Found

That is the medal. Now the other story -- the one Bober would actually defend if you pushed him at eleven at night -- which is that the beer helps.

There is a study for that. Everyone has read about it, or read about someone reading about it. Jarosz, Colflesh and Wiley, 2012, "Uncorking the muse," in *Consciousness and Cognition*, published as a Short Communication. Forty male social drinkers, twenty per cell, recruited on Craigslist, given 100-proof Smirnoff at 0.88 grams per kilo with bagels and a screening of *Ratatouille*, brought to a blood alcohol of 0.075, then tested on the Remote Associates Test: fifteen word-association items, one minute each. The drinkers got 0.58 correct against 0.42. p = .001. A large effect.

What the headlines left out: no placebo, no blinding. Every man in that room knew whether he had been drinking. And the much-repeated finding that the insights arrived as sudden flashes was marginal at p < .08; only a narrower count reached p = .039.

Then somebody ran it properly. Benedek and Zohrer, 2020, "Creativity on tap 2": preregistered, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, N = 125, three groups at placebo, 0.03 and 0.06 blood alcohol. On the Remote Associates Test: F(2,122) = 0.02, p = .98. Not a small effect. Not a weak effect. A flat line. No effect on divergent thinking either, and -- awkwardly for the original -- no working-memory impairment, which was the mechanism Jarosz had proposed.

The authors' own summary: they "do not replicate previous findings reporting positive effects of alcohol on RAT performance," and "the empirical effect should be hard to miss in a well-powered study."

Two details finish it. The Remote Associates Test has an internal consistency of .45 and .46 and a retest correlation of .32 -- a very noisy instrument, the sort that throws off enormous published effects and then cannot find them again. And the null study was funded by the Dutch Beer Institute. A beer-funded experiment reporting that beer does nothing is not biased in the direction you would expect.

## The Only Study That Ever Looked At A Picture

One experiment in the entire literature tested alcohol on a visual creative task rather than a word game: Lapp, Collins and Izzo, 1994, in the *American Journal of Psychology*. Balanced placebo design -- crossing what people actually drank with what they were told they had drunk -- on arranging pictures of wildflowers by colour, shape and number.

The most useful sentence in this chapter. There was "no pharmacological effect of alcohol on the creative combinations that subjects produced," but novelty was "enhanced when subjects thought they had consumed alcohol, whether or not they had actually done so."

The ethanol did nothing. The belief did the work.

Same shape as the ribbon. The object is inert; the story attached to it is the active ingredient. A blue ribbon certifies nothing -- it certifies because people agreed it does. A 4-zloty lager does not unlock associative thinking; it unlocks because the man holding it has read that it does.

Now the honest caveat, because a chapter that debunks has to meet the standard it enforces. The strongest version of the expectancy argument is Kate Fox's: drunken comportment follows cultural expectation, not chemistry -- people behave the way their society has taught them drunk people behave. It is a good argument and it fits everything above. It also has to be said plainly that Fox's drink books were published by industry bodies -- the Portman Group in 1992, the Brewers and Licensed Retailers Association in 1996 -- and that the BBC disclosed her drinks-industry funding. Say it, do not bury it. And the counter-evidence is real: Bushman and Cooper, 1990, found that alcohol does cause aggression. Not expects. Causes. Ethanol is a drug and it does things.

It is just that none of the things it does is painting.

## Nobody Has Ever Asked A Painter

A systematic Europe PMC search for experimental work on alcohol and visual artists returns zero studies. Not few. Zero. Everything in the literature is verbal -- word games you can administer with a clipboard. The people who stand on lifts have never been in a laboratory.

The closest anyone has come is Roe, 1946, reported through Ludwig, 1990: seventeen established artists, interviewed. All of them drank. "All but one avoided drinking while painting." None used alcohol to get past a technical problem. Ludwig's gloss is the whole argument in nine words: alcohol "provided the freedom for painting but impaired the discipline."

Seventeen painters. A tiny number, and the only number there is, and all of them said the same thing -- not that drink is evil, but that it is for afterwards.

And the slogan everybody quotes here is not even correctly attributed. "Write drunk, edit sober" is Peter De Vries, *Reuben, Reuben*, 1964, page 242, spoken by a fictional dissolute poet named Gowan McGland, and the original is symmetrical and much less flattering to your evening: "Sometimes I write drunk and revise sober, and sometimes I write sober and revise drunk. But you have to have both elements in creation -- the Apollonian and the Dionysian, or spontaneity and restraint, emotion and discipline." It was pinned on Hemingway in a 2005 Usenet post to rec.woodworking. Hemingway, asked directly, said the opposite: "Have you ever heard of anyone who drank while he worked? You're thinking of Faulkner. He does sometimes -- and I can tell right in the middle of a page when he's had his first one."

The maxim is a half-sentence, amputated, wearing a dead novelist's name. It is the same object as the can: a real thing with a fake medal on it.

## What Is Left

So: the ribbon is decoration promoted to a trophy in the 1950s. The beer is Polish. The company is cutting staff and subletting its own office. The study is a Short Communication with forty men and no placebo, and the replication came back at p = .98. The only visual experiment ever run found belief, not ethanol. The only painters ever asked said don't.

Both flattering stories are gone, and the joke was never on the man holding the can. It was on the story, written by people who needed him to believe it and who did not know his name.

But something is still happening at half past three. The light does go flat. The wall does stop being readable. He does stop, he does open it, and ten minutes later he goes back to work having decided something. That is not the ribbon and it is not the ethanol, and we have not named it yet.

It is smaller than a muse. It is also sadder, and better, and it is on the lift.
