# Ten Minutes Nobody Pays For

## The Invoice

Start with the paperwork, because the answer has been sitting in the paperwork the whole time.

An exterior commercial mural in Poland is priced by the square metre. Two hundred to a thousand
zloty per square metre, with the rate dropping toward the two hundred end once you are above a
hundred and fifty square metres, because scale is a discount. A job over a hundred square metres
starts at twenty thousand zloty. Works of art take eight percent VAT rather than the full rate,
which means the tax authority has a category for this and the category is generous. Good Looking
Studio, the market leader, will not publish a price list at all, which tells you the number is
negotiable and that the negotiating is done somewhere the public is not invited.

Two things about those numbers before anybody gets excited on Bober's behalf. They are
client-side contractor prices, not artist take-home: out of them come the paint, the lift, the
licensed operator of the lift, the insurance, the assistants, and the months of design and permit
work that nobody pays for separately. And Poland has no published muralist day rate. There is no
interview anywhere in which a Polish muralist states their own fee. A trade that covers the sides
of tower blocks in a country of thirty-seven million has left no public record of what it pays
the person holding the spray can. Make of that what you like. The muralists apparently have.

What matters here is not the size of the number but the unit attached to it. The unit is the
square metre. Not the day, not the hour, not the idea. Surface.

Everything the invoice recognises is a surface or a thing that touches a surface. A thousand
square metre gable takes something like a hundred and seventy to three hundred and thirty litres
of base paint for two coats, before a single line of the actual image gets sprayed. That is
billable, and it should be, because paint costs money and somebody has to carry it. The
podnosnik koszowy is billable, and so is the man in it, who must hold UDT certification, because
the state has decided -- correctly -- that people forty feet above a pavement should be operated
by somebody with a document. The season is billable in the sense that it constrains everything:
acrylic facade paint wants plus ten to plus thirty, silicone will go down to plus five, and rain,
wind, frost, humidity and hard direct sun all veto the day, which leaves Poland with an exterior
painting season of roughly six to seven months. Half the year the wall is simply not available.
That, too, is on the schedule, priced in as risk.

Now the other column. The looking is not billable. The deciding is not billable. The two months
of drawings before anybody agrees on anything is not billable, and neither is the tour of
permissions -- the owner or the wspolnota mieszkaniowa, then the city, then the konserwator
zabytkow if the building is listed, then whatever the local uchwala krajobrazowa has to say about
it. That can run months, and it runs at nobody's expense but his.

And the standing back and hating it is not billable. There is no line on any Polish mural invoice
that reads: three-quarters of an hour, forty feet up, working out that the left side is dead.

## What The Can Is Actually For

Here is where the book stops agreeing with everybody else in the file.

Every source that has an opinion about Bober and his beer is arguing about what the alcohol does
to him. The romantics say it opens him up. The scientists say it does not. The scientists win,
and they win badly. Lapp, Collins and Izzo ran the only alcohol experiment ever conducted on a
visual creative task -- arranging wildflower pictures by colour, shape and number, which is not
mural painting but is at least in the right sensory postcode -- and they ran it as a balanced
placebo design, crossing what people actually drank with what people were told they had drunk.
The finding, in their words: "no pharmacological effect of alcohol on the creative combinations
that subjects produced," but novelty "enhanced when subjects thought they had consumed alcohol,
whether or not they had actually done so."

The ethanol did nothing. The belief did everything.

Everybody who has quoted that result has quoted it as a debunking, and it is one, but they have
all stopped one step too early. The interesting question is not whether belief is doing the work.
It is which belief.

The assumed belief is: this will make me better. That is the belief the muse story requires, and
it is the belief the replication crisis has been busy dismantling for a decade. But that is not
the only thing a man can believe about a can, and on a lift at half past three it is not even the
most useful thing to believe.

The operative belief is: I am allowed to not be working right now.

That is a permission, not a stimulant. And permission is the scarcest commodity on the entire
job, because look at what else is on that lift. The paint has a temperature range and does not
care about him. The weather has a veto and does not care about him. The lift has a certificate
and a fuel gauge. The client has a completion date. The wspolnota has opinions about the
scaffolding blocking the bins. The season is closing at the far end of October whether the wall
is finished or not. Every constraint in Bober's day is external, physical and non-negotiable, and
every one of them is priced.

The can is the only object in the basket that answers to him. It is also the only object in the
basket that is legible to everyone else. A man standing still on a lift staring at a wall is a
man not working, and in a trade paid by the square metre, not working is the one activity with no
defence. A man standing still on a lift with a beer in his hand is a man on a break. Same posture.
Same forty feet. Same wall. Completely different accounting.

He is not buying inspiration. He is buying an alibi for the ten minutes the invoice does not
mention.

## The Worse Version Of A Free Thing

The argument has an ugly corollary and the book is not going to hide it.

If what he actually needed were the loose associative state -- ideas drifting sideways into each
other, the left side of the wall suddenly rhyming with something he saw on a bus in 2019 -- there
is a mechanism that reliably delivers that and it is free. It is REM sleep. Cai's people showed
REM specifically improving performance on remote-association problems. Wagner's sleepers were
more than twice as likely to spot a hidden shortcut in a task they had been grinding at awake.
And Ebrahim's review of alcohol and sleep gives the trade in one sentence: "The onset of the first
REM sleep period is significantly delayed at all doses."

At all doses. Not at drunk. At all of them.

So the drink buys an hour of a diluted version of what the night hands over for nothing, and then
charges for it by postponing the night. This is the book's own inference and it has to be labelled
as one: nobody has run the study. No experiment has tested the alcohol-versus-REM trade-off in
creative work, and anyone who tells you otherwise is filling a gap with confidence.

But notice what the inference does to the muse story even unproven. If the beer were really about
the associative state, it would be a bad instrument badly used, a man paying rent on something he
already owns. That only stops being stupid once you accept that the associative state was never
the purchase. The purchase was the stop.

Ludwig, looking at thirty-four heavy-drinking creative achievers over whole careers, found that
alcohol directly decreased output in twenty-six of them -- seventy-six and a half percent -- and
directly increased it in three. His own summary of the mess: "No simplistic conclusions are
possible." Fine. Take the unsimplistic one. Over a career, the drink takes more than it gives.
Over ten minutes on a Tuesday, it gives him the only thing on offer, which is the right to stand
there.

## A Language With A Word For Everything Except This

Polish is a language that names drinking with forensic precision, and the vocabulary makes the
argument better than the argument does.

There is kolega od kieliszka, a dictionary entry, PAN's own, defined as a person with whom close
acquaintance rests on meetings at which alcohol is drunk. Poland has a lexicalised term for the
friend you only have because you drink together. There is strzemienny, the stirrup cup, the last
round before the group disperses, historically served in a kulawka -- the lame one -- a glass with
no base, which cannot be set down until it is empty. There is the living modern form of that same
idea, rozchodniak, and its diminutive rozchodniaczek, which is a small affectionate word for the
drink that is supposed to end the drinking. There is oblewac, to douse a thing, to wet an
occasion. There is zakaska, from kasac, to bite: the bite that follows the swallow, named after
its own timing.

Every one of those words exists because the culture needed to mark a boundary and used alcohol to
mark it. The last one. The one that ends it. The one that turns pan into a first name. The
grammar of the day, administered in glasses.

Now go looking for the Polish word for an hour of unpaid thinking about a job you are already
being underpaid to do, and come back with nothing, because there is nothing. No dictionary has an
entry for it. No invoice has a line for it. No client has ever asked for a quote on it. The single
most important activity in making a wall that is not garbage has no name, no price and no social
form.

So it borrows one. The can is not a muse and it is not a weakness. It is a receipt for time that
has no invoice -- the only permission structure a man in this trade can carry up a lift in one
hand.

## The Joke Is On Him And He Knows It

By now Bober has read his own can, which is a thing this book has done to him and not for him.

The ribbon commemorates a prize nobody was awarded. The slogan about 1893 was written in the
1950s. The reputation for not advertising began as a man in 1985 deciding a dying brand was not
worth the advertising money. The brewery that made the thing in his hand is at Rakszawa in
Podkarpackie, an hour and a bit from places he has painted, and Van Pur's own copy boasts that
most of the beer brewed there "is exported to highly demanding and distant foreign markets."
Whatever else is in the can, some of it came back.

Szczerek got to this shape first, and got to it harder. In Mordor a girl on a train points at the
narrator's bottle and asks him why he is drinking impotence medicine so ostentatiously, in front
of everyone. The prop is exposed as a joke on the man holding it. The chapter does not end with
him putting it down. It ends with him handing it over: "Here. Drink up."

That is the correct ending and this book is going to copy it, because the alternative endings are
all lies. Knowing the ribbon is fake does not move half past three by a single minute. The light
still flattens. The colour still stops being judgeable. The invoice still has no line for looking.
A false medal is a perfectly good excuse if what you needed was an excuse.

And there is a version of this where the beer wins and he loses, where the ten minutes becomes an
afternoon and the afternoon becomes the reason the phone stops ringing and his liver files its own
opinion; the Polish mortality tables have a column shaped exactly like a man his age, and this
book is not going to pretend otherwise. That is the sentence. It has been paid.

What Szczerek also supplies is the refusal to be ashamed of the object, which is the last thing
the argument needs. Every country makes icons out of its own space, he says, Americans with their
roadside diners and their fire escapes, so why not here -- and culture.pl's gloss on the end of
Siodemka is the whole position in nine words: it may be ugly but it's ours. Apply it to the can.
A Polish lager brewed in a Podkarpackie village, sold in Polomarket at about four zloty, wearing
the costume of an American beer that a British bottle shop labels USA Trash Lager and a Chinese
importer sells at forty-four dollars with a champagne flute. That is not a defeat. That is the
most local possible object in the most foreign possible label, which happens to be an exact
description of a Polish mural festival, a Polish supermarket, and a Polish artist on a Polish lift.

## Mural Nie Istnieje

One more thing on the ledger and then we can leave him alone.

The wall is going to die. Not the paint's fault -- outdoor sacrificial anti-graffiti coatings last
about a year, which is a maintenance schedule rather than a lifespan, and the paint underneath
will outlive the coating for as long as the building stands. That is the problem. Walls in Poland
do not die of weather. They die of demolition and of thermomodernisation, which is what it is
called when a gable gets wrapped in insulation and a mural becomes something under the styrofoam.

Odzyski went up in Lodz in 2014, five artists, called one of the largest murals in Europe. The
project page for it now says three words. Mural nie istnieje. The mural does not exist.

So here is the full accounting for the object Bober is making. It is priced by the square metre.
The thinking that determines whether it is any good is unpaid. The season allows it to be made
during roughly half the year. Its protective coating needs replacing annually. And its ultimate
cause of death is a municipal insulation grant.

Against that, ten minutes.

## Forty Feet

Half past three, and the light has gone flat the way it does, everything on the wall turning the
same value, the reds and the greys agreeing with each other for the first time all day, which is
the eye announcing it is done and will not be taking further questions.

Two-thirds finished. The right side is doing what he wanted. The left side is not, and he has
known that for about forty minutes, and knowing it and being able to name it are separated by
exactly the interval that nobody is paying for.

He opens the can. It makes the noise. The ribbon on it is not a prize, the year on it is not the
year, the address on the back is a village in Podkarpackie, and the beer inside got two out of
ten from people who taste beer for a living. He knows all of it now. It changes nothing, because
none of that was ever the job the can was doing.

The basket sways a little, the way they do. Down on the street somebody is trying to park badly.
The lift operator with his certificate is on the phone to somebody about tomorrow. There are maybe
two weeks left in the season and then the paint stops being legal against the wall.

He drinks, and he looks, and nobody is paying him for either.

The wall gets the ten minutes.
