# Translation brief -- Rakszawa, Wisconsin

You are translating a comic nonfiction book about Lukasz Bober, a Polish
mixed-media artist and muralist who drinks Pabst Blue Ribbon. Register: funny
the way a very smart friend is funny at 2am -- rigorous, digressive,
occasionally devastating. The stereotype is always the target; Bober and
Poland never are.

## The rule that matters most

Translate the JOKE, not the words. Where a pun or rhythm will not carry,
build an equivalent joke in the target language that does the same work in
the same place. A literal translation that is not funny has failed. A free
translation that lands has succeeded.

## Absolute constraints

1. NEVER change a fact, number, date, statistic, proper name, brand or
   citation. 11.51 L stays 11.51 L. Van Pur stays Van Pur. Rakszawa stays
   Rakszawa. Benedek and Zohrer's p = .98 stays p = .98.
2. Preserve markdown structure exactly: one "# " chapter title, "## " section
   breaks in the same positions, same paragraph breaks.
3. TRANSLATE the chapter title and all section headings. They are vivid and
   concrete; keep them vivid and concrete, not literal-clumsy.
4. Do not add, cut or reorder arguments. Same claims, same order, same
   evidence.
5. Do not add translator's notes, footnotes or brackets explaining yourself.

## Punctuation and encoding

- Output UTF-8 with FULL correct diacritics. The English edition is ASCII-only
  by design; that rule does NOT apply to you and stripping accents would be an
  error.
- Use " -- " for the dash, as in the source. Straight quotes only, no curly
  quotes, no ellipsis character (use three dots).

## Polish-language material inside the text

The book quotes Polish sources. The English edition transliterates them into
ASCII (e.g. "Poniewierac ma, a nie smakowac"). You must restore the correct
original spelling with diacritics: "Poniewierac ma, a nie smakowac" ->
"Poniewierac" becomes the properly accented Polish form. Do this for every
Polish quote, proverb, and dictionary term. Get the diacritics right; a Polish
reader will notice instantly.

### If you are translating into FRENCH
Keep every Polish quote in Polish, correctly accented, followed by a natural
French rendering in the same sentence or the next one, the way a good French
essayist handles a foreign quotation. Do not drop the Polish.

### If you are translating into POLISH -- READ THIS TWICE
The English book explains Polish words to foreigners. In Polish that is
absurd and condescending. Therefore:
- DELETE glosses that merely translate Polish into Polish. Do not write the
  Polish equivalent of "kac, which means hangover."
- KEEP everything that is genuinely informative to a Polish reader: the
  etymologies (kac from German Katzenjammer; melina from Yiddish), the fact
  that a term is a PAN dictionary headword, the grammatical observations (that
  "urwal sie film" makes the film the subject so you are not the agent), the
  historical dates, and the fact that Polish has a diminutive for the drink
  meant to end the drinking.
- Where the English says "the Polish word for X", rewrite so it reads as a
  Pole talking to Poles about their own language -- observation, not tuition.
- Polish quotes appear simply as themselves, with no gloss at all.
- The tone toward Poland must stay warm and self-aware. In Polish this book
  reads as a Pole's own joke about his country, never as a foreigner's.

## Quality bar

Read your finished chapter once and ask: would a native speaker know this was
translated? If yes, fix the sentences that gave it away. Prose rhythm is short
declarative punch, expand, expand, short punch. Keep it.
