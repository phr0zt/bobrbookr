# What the Dues Did Not Take, the Vodka Took

## The right to be the only bar in the world

In 1496 the Sejm passed a statute, and a Polish landowner acquired the exclusive right to
produce and sell beer, gorzalka and mead within his own estate. A royal grant in 1572 widened
it. By the seventeenth century it was ordinary, unremarkable, the way the countryside worked.
The name for it is propinacja.

Read the terms slowly. They are better than any modern business plan.

The lord makes the alcohol. The lord owns the only place it may legally be sold, the karczma,
where anyone else's alcohol is banned. The lord holds the import privilege too, so there is no
legal route around him. His customers cannot leave the estate. He is also, in practice, the local
court. And his income from propinacja often exceeded his income from every other activity on the
land put together.

Not from grain. Not from timber. From the karczma.

Prince Czartoryski had a phrase for what he was looking at, quoted by Kacper Poblocki in
*Chamstwo*: a real mint. Poblocki's own sentence is the one to keep, because it does the
arithmetic on the whole arrangement. "Three hundred years of serfdom were also three hundred
years of the consistent getting-drunk of the Polish people. Vodka became the most profitable
branch of the lordly economy."

Nobody drifted into that. Somebody built it, wrote it down, and enforced it.

## The quota, and the vodka left on the doorstep

Monopoly is half the machine. The other half has a Polish name that sounds like a tax code entry
and works like a boot: *przymus propinacyjny*, the obligation on a peasant household to buy the
alcohol produced on the lord's estate, and *przymus konsumpcyjny*, which fixed the annual
quantity.

Three documented forms. You could not buy alcohol your lord had not made. Or you distilled your
own and paid him by volume for the privilege. Or -- the version that should end any conversation
about Polish drinking as a folk custom -- you had to take a set quota of vodka or okowita, and
whatever you left was delivered anyway, poured out in front of your house, and charged to you.

The consequence recorded on the Polish side of the record is small and completely devastating.
People in the countryside avoided holding weddings, because a wedding triggered an excessive
vodka quota from the estate.

Sit with that. A peasant does not marry off a daughter this year, because the party is a taxable
event and the tax is measured in litres. Reymont's line in *Chlopi* -- take a christening with
water, drench a wedding with vodka, and a funeral with weeping -- reads as folklore. It is also a
list of the three occasions on which estate revenue reliably spiked.

Karl Grolmann said it in one sentence, and it is the spine of everything that follows: *czego nie
wydarto chlopu daninami, panszczyzna, odbierano posrednio przez wodke*. What was not torn from
the peasant by dues and corvee was taken indirectly through vodka.

Two collection systems, one peasant. The direct one took the grain and the labour. The indirect
one sold the grain back as spirit and took the rest. There is no third pocket.

## The fable, and who spread it

The propinacja right was often leased rather than run directly -- to village heads, innkeepers,
and very frequently to Jewish *arendarze*, who worked the tavern and paid the noble his cut. This
is where a lie has traditionally been inserted, and the book is not walking past it.

The fable is that calculated Jewish sobriety enabled a predatory monopoly over Christian
peasants. It was propagated for centuries, and one of its busiest distribution channels was
temperance-preaching Catholic clergy. The lie had a job: it moved the blame from the man who
owned the monopoly to the man licensed to stand behind its counter.

Glenn Dynner took it apart from inside the archive in *Yankel's Tavern* (Oxford, 2014),
documenting substantial Jewish alcoholism -- roughly five thousand *kvitlekh*, petition notes, in
the YIVO holdings -- and showing the tavern as a place where Poles and Jews mixed rather than a
trap set for one by the other.

The structure is what matters here. The noble held the right. The lessee carried the risk, the
hours, and eventually the accusation. Clerical temperance and antisemitic scapegoating grew up
entangled, and the tangle was useful to exactly one party.

## The oath, and the petition against sermons

Here is the thing nobody who repeats the stereotype has ever heard.

Between 1844 and 1855, hundreds of thousands of Galician serfs took a public, solemn oath to stop
drinking. In some districts as many as three quarters of the adults did it. Not a pamphlet
campaign, not a resolution passed by a committee. Three quarters of the grown population of a
district, standing up in front of each other and swearing off.

In the Prussian partition the Brotherhood of Sobriety had started in Silesia in 1844 around
Fr Jan Alojzy Ficek, with papal approval from Pius IX in 1851. In the Russian partition from
September 1858 the pledge took three quarters of the population of Grodno, Kaunas and Vilnius,
and sales fell 33 percent in Grodno, 40 in Vilnius, 70 in Kaunas.

So the answer to "why do Poles drink" cannot be "because Poles want to." When the chance came,
an enormous number of them publicly and collectively did not.

What happened next should be taught in schools.

The lords defended propinacja as, in their own words, a fixed, integral, indispensable part of
their income. And they petitioned for a ban on sermons trespassing on the relation of serfdom.

The peasants organised against the drink, and the men profiting from the drink tried to make the
sermons illegal.

In the Russian partition the same instinct arrived with an army attached. The finance ministry, in
Mark Lawrence Schrad's account, "demanded that the Catholics renounce their temperance heresy and
preach as the Orthodox did: that vodka was a 'harmless' and even 'necessary' indulgence."
Taverns were attacked, troops deployed, protesters flogged and force-fed liquor through funnels.
Over 780 people were tried before military tribunals and exiled to Siberia. Temperance societies
were outlawed in 1863. (That the ban landed in the year of the January Uprising is our inference
about what a state fears, not a published finding.)

A British journalist of the period wrote the sentence that survives all of it: "The teetotalers
were flogged into drinking... These things sound incredible, but they are true."

Men were beaten for not drinking. Not in a folk tale. In the nineteenth century, with paperwork.

## Three abolitions, because there were three partitions

Propinacja did not end. It ended three times, on three calendars, because Poland was being
administered by three revenue departments.

Prussian partition, 1845. Galicia, 1889 -- where it effectively persisted anyway, and an 1905 law
scheduled the real finish for 1911. Russian partition, 1898.

Half a century between the first date and the last, on land that is now one country. Which is why
the alcohol-law map of the nineteenth century is still legible: those partition lines are the
lines Szczerek is pointing at when he says Polska A and Polska B. Nobody redrew them. They just
stopped being printed.

## The mechanism, stated cleanly, in Russian

The clearest statement of how this machine works is about Russia, and it has to be labelled that
way, loudly, because it is not about Poland at all.

Ivan the Terrible imported the *kabak* from Kazan in 1552. The Law Code of 1649 that bound the
peasant to the land also banned selling vodka off it: one law, tying the man and his liquor
supply to the same square of dirt. By the middle of the nineteenth century the monopoly ran to
over a third of imperial revenue and covered 100 percent of the army's operating budget.

Schrad's conclusion: "Vodka became synonymous with Russian culture not because the Russian people
demanded it, but because the Russian state supplied it." And the consequence for any government
thinking of acting: "If the state were to encourage the health and sobriety of its citizens, it
would face immediate bankruptcy. The lesson was clear." Vasily Kokorev, most infamous of the tax
farmers, named what the auctions really sold: "an exemption from the rules."

Schrad never wrote about Poland. Poland is not among the countries in his archival sources. The
parallel here is ours and nobody else's, drawn because the Polish documents describe the same
arrangement two centuries earlier at estate scale instead of imperial scale.

His cross-national table closes the last comfortable escape route: liquor taxes ran to 26 percent
of state income in tsarist Russia, 25 in the United States, 23 in Britain, 20 in the German
Empire, 15 in Sweden and Belgium, 11 in France and Norway, 9 in Switzerland and Austria-Hungary.

The United States. Twenty-five percent. Whatever this is, it is not a Slavic personality defect.
It is what a treasury does after discovering a product that is cheap to make, addictive to buy,
and hard to hide from a man with a ledger.

## The word still painted on the shop

The Second Republic did not dismantle the logic, it nationalised it. The Panstwowy Monopol
Spirytusowy was founded in 1924, and the state took over the business the nobles had run.

Which is why, a hundred years later, in a market economy inside the European Union, the ordinary
Polish word for an off-licence is *sklep monopolowy*. The monopoly shop.

The monopoly is gone. The sign is not. Every Polish town centre carries a piece of 1924 in its
windows, and nobody notices, the way nobody notices a fossil in a paving slab.

## The shop that opened at ten and could sell nothing until one

The People's Republic ran the same instrument with the polarity flipped.

In the 1980s a rule banned alcohol sales before 13:00, while the monopolowy shops typically
opened at 10:00. A shop selling nothing but alcohol, staffed, lit, unlocked, and legally
forbidden to sell anything at all for the first three hours of its day. Somebody stood behind
that counter every morning being paid to not do the only thing the building was for.

Then, from 13 December 1981, alcohol ration cards, inside a rationing system reaching a scope
described as considerably wider than under the German occupation. Outsiders always get the
mechanic wrong: the card did not buy the bottle. It only entitled you to buy the bottle. You
still paid, in ordinary money, at the ordinary price. The state had found a way to charge full
price for a permission slip.

Across the border the engineering was more exact still. A Soviet decree of December 1958 banned
the sale of vodka by the glass, so drinkers were now forced to buy a whole bottle and go looking
for chance companions. After the 1961 reform a half-litre cost 2 roubles 87 kopecks -- one rouble
each from three men, 13 kopecks over. With no matching glasses they poured *po bulkam*, by the
glugs. By the early 1970s vodka cost 3.62, the arithmetic broke, and the custom carried on
anyway.

That ritual is Russian, not Polish, whatever the internet tells you. Poland had the whip-round
and the wetting of a thing that had happened -- *oblewamy?*, so, are we dousing it? The point is
not the ritual. The point is that a finance ministry designed one by accident and people kept
performing the design decades after the price that caused it stopped existing.

Kazik Staszewski put the whole PRL fiscal logic in one line: whoever does not drink vodka is a
subversive, knowingly depleting state revenue, a man without ideals.

That is a joke. It is also the accounting.

## The one time it was the workers who noticed

Jacek Moskalewicz wrote the Polish version of this thesis, about his own lifetime. "The Solidarity
movement already had on its banners the alcohol question in the
early 1980s. The working class apparently blamed the government for pushing alcohol to manipulate
drunken society and to discredit its demands for a better life and freedom."

Alcohol supply, on the banners, next to the wages and the free press.

Out of those negotiations came the Act of 26 October 1982 on upbringing in sobriety and
counteracting alcoholism -- still the governing law, the one banning alcohol advertising with
beer carved out of it. By the end of the decade consumption had levelled off at around six litres
of ethanol a head.

It worked. Which is why it was destroyed.

## The reversal, which is the punchline

Moskalewicz again, on what happened after 1989: the control system was "totally rejected as a
remnant of socialism, and most restrictions were lifted."

Licensing went to local councils, whose budgets were fed by the licence fees. Councils were now
paid per outlet. Outlets went from around 40,000 in the late 1980s to more than 200,000 within a
few years. The brewing industry expanded capacity and doubled, then tripled, consumption in under
a decade. Drinking among 15 and 16 year olds doubled.

Then, in 2002, parliament cut excise on spirits by 30 percent. Spirits went from 1.7 litres of
pure alcohol per head in 2002 to 3.4 in 2008. Zatonski and colleagues find that Poland's long
post-communist health improvement halted in 2002, with alcohol-attributable mortality rising from
7.5 to 17.8 per 100,000 between 2002 and 2019, and 130,000 cumulative deaths from causes that are
100 percent alcohol-attributable across that span. 2019 alone: 10,396 people.

Schrad has one flattering paragraph about modern Poland -- spirits down from 61 percent of
consumption at the fall of communism to 26 percent by 2002, male life expectancy up four full
years, against a Russia going the other way. Those numbers are real. He stopped the clock in
2002, the exact year Poland cut the tax and turned around, and the Polish literature credits the
earlier shift to a deregulated brewing industry rather than to any plan.

By 2023 the international assessment, from Rehm and colleagues in *Lancet Regional Health --
Europe*, was that Polish alcohol control had been "mostly dismantled."

The residue, in numbers: a Polish minimum wage bought 32 half-litre bottles of vodka in 2001 and
107 in 2021. There were 119,598 alcohol retail outlets in 2024, one for every 302 residents, in a
country with one pharmacy per 2,700.

Nine to one against the pharmacy. Nobody voted for that ratio. It was assembled licence fee by
licence fee by people whose budgets depended on it -- a sentence that would have made perfect
sense to a Galician landlord in 1780.

## And now, the swing back

The machine is being re-tuned again, in the same way, from the same direction.

Krakow has banned night sales city-wide since 1 July 2023, and alcohol-related police
interventions fell 48.5 percent in the first year and 61.8 in the second. Warsaw goes city-wide
from 1 June 2026, 22:00 to 06:00, passed 57 votes to 2, sole exemption the airport duty free.
Excise rose 5 percent on 1 January 2026 rather than the planned 15, because President Nawrocki
vetoed the larger rise on 18 December 2025: "I cannot agree to an increase in excise tax
introduced without time for entrepreneurs and consumers to prepare."

A total ban on all alcohol advertising, beer and 0.0 percent included, fines to a million zloty,
cleared the Health Committee in July 2026 and awaits a second reading. It is not law. If it
becomes law, an alcohol-brand mural becomes illegal -- worth holding on to, because somebody in
this book paints walls for a living.

## Which brings us back to the lift

None of this was Lukasz Bober's decision.

He did not sit in the Sejm in 1496, lease a karczma, or petition anybody to ban a sermon. He was
not in Silesia in 1844 taking an oath, or in the Russian partition being flogged for keeping one.
He did not found the monopoly in 1924, or stand behind a counter from ten until one forbidden to
sell. He did not vote for the 30 percent cut in 2002. Nobody has ever asked him how many shops
per resident he would like, and nobody has asked you either.

What he has is a country where the word for the shop is still the word for the monopoly, the shop
is closer than the pharmacy, the drink costs less against a day's work than it did when his
parents were young, and six centuries of very competent people made very good money keeping all
three of those things true.

So the can is within arm's reach on the lift at half past three. Of course it is. It has been
engineered to be within arm's reach of a Pole for six hundred years.

The only free choice left in the whole apparatus is which can. And that one -- an American label
on a beer brewed in Podkarpackie -- turns out to be a costume, and a lie, and the subject of the
next chapter.
